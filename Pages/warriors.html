Pits that were uncovered

These pits housed the stone army, stone horses, and living quarters of the Qin emperor's terracotta warriors.

Pit 1

The largest pit (14,260 square meters). It holds the main battle formation of an estimated 6,000 warriors and horses, featuring parallel corridors with infantry and chariots.

Pit 2

A more complex, highly diverse unit covering 6,000 square meters. It contains a mixed force of cavalry, archers/crossbowmen, infantry, and chariots.

Pit 3

The smallest command post (520 square meters). It represents the upper military headquarters with high-ranking officers and a single chariot.

The Discovery of the Terracotta Army

In March 1974, local farmers digging a well near Xi'an, China stumbled upon fragments of pottery that would lead to one of the most remarkable archaeological discoveries of the modern era. What they had found were the remains of the Terracotta Army, an ancient legion of life-sized warriors created over 2,000 years ago. The discovery revealed thousands of intricately crafted clay soldiers, horses, and chariots that had been buried to protect China's First Emperor in the afterlife .

These extraordinary artifacts were commissioned by Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor of unified China, during the 3rd century BCE. The Terracotta Army consists of thousands of life-sized figures arranged in military formation, each warrior individually crafted with unique facial features, armor, and expressions. The level of detail and craftsmanship demonstrated by these ancient artisans provides an unparalleled window into the military organization, technology, and artistic achievements of ancient China.

Creation and Historical Significance

The Terracotta Army was constructed under the reign of Qin Shi Huang (259-210 BCE), the visionary ruler who unified China for the first time in history . Approximately 700,000 workers labored for nearly four decades to create this monumental underground army. The project reflected the emperor's belief that he would need protection and military might in the afterlife, a concept deeply rooted in ancient Chinese philosophy and burial practices.

Each terracotta warrior was meticulously crafted from local clay, with individualized faces, hairstyles, and armor designs that reflected the diverse ranks and roles within an actual army. The soldiers were arranged in three large pits discovered near Xi'an, positioned as if ready for battle. Beyond the warriors, the complex also included terracotta horses, chariots, officials, musicians, and acrobats—a complete microcosm of the imperial court and military force. This UNESCO World Heritage Site stands as a testament to the organizational prowess, artistic sophistication, and ambition of the Qin Dynasty, forever preserving the vision of one of history's greatest emperors.
